# Assumptions and unresolved research questions

The implementation is verified on synthetic networks. It has no real-world flood, bus-operation or passenger validation. The checks establish agreement with the stated snapshot model, not that the supplied safety rules or preference rates are appropriate in practice.

1. One bus, one direction of an original route, one current snapshot. Repeated updates occur at committed event boundaries. Future water levels, times and demand changes are not forecast. A route safe in one snapshot can become unsafe later.
2. Transit safety, road usability, boarding accessibility and safe waiting locations are supplied independently. Roads require legal bus access and turning compatibility in the road-state representation. The prototype cannot certify vehicle clearance, flood depth, pedestrian safety or emergency rescue.
3. Current unsafe locations permit only explicitly certified escape roads to safe transit nodes and a reachable approved finish. No such action produces intervention_required. This does not claim that every physical emergency has a modeled safe solution.
4. All original intermediate services are optional. Weights encourage service but never guarantee it. A zero-demand hospital earns zero reward regardless of its priority; a separate essential-service benefit would require an agreed change.
5. Original service order is relaxed. Review whether Sir wants directional progression, limited backtracking or a service-order constraint. Repeated physical road nodes/edges are permitted, while each stop's demand is served at most once. The original terminal is absorbing so terminal arrival equals finishing there. These policy assumptions are material.
6. Stop-access demand is a service proxy attached to disjoint groups per trip. No vehicle capacity, passenger destination, actual boarding, delivery, transfers, queue, walking reassignment or overlapping-catchment correction. Remaining demand means unserved opportunities; already served demand is not automatically replenished at the next snapshot.
7. Current-stop dwell is all or nothing at service-event boundaries. A partly executed service/edge must be represented by an explicit remaining state; it is not silently approximated. A terminal reached safely completes the trip.
8. Full-trip cumulative hinges preserve the latest written interpretation. Kappa and rates require operator calibration. The examples are illustrative. Score comparisons across states must account for excluded fixed historical reward and included elapsed penalties.
9. The finite supplied stop universe and optional normal-time corridor restrict scope. The exact result is conditional on those candidates. Candidate discovery from geographic data or accessibility catchments remains future work.
10. Static shortest connections assume nonnegative additive times, no path-dependent benefit, and permitted physical repeats. Slower alternatives with special safety or turning properties must already be represented in the road graph; otherwise shortest-path reduction is insufficient.
11. Exact subset dynamic programming has exponential complexity. Limits return an audited feasible incumbent and a loose bound; the interface can pause during the synchronous solve. Large network/candidate performance requires later benchmarking and possibly an MILP/heuristic implementation under the same audit contract. No external solver optimality gap is fabricated.
12. Flood data, observed travel-time distributions, demand, safe endpoints and operator penalty calibration are missing for a real case. No fleet, depot, dispatch, prediction or digital-twin components were added.

Questions for the meeting are operational choices rather than blockers for this reproducible prototype: acceptable service order/backtracking; safe endpoint and emergency-escape certification; meaning and overlap of stop demand; full-trip thresholds; and operator trade-off ranges for priorities and cumulative rates.
